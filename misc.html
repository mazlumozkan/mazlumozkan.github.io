Autobiography

I grew up in Altınyurt, a small village in Gaziantep, Türkiye. Village life was quiet, but films and books opened doors to worlds far beyond it. Through them I travelled to other countries, heard other languages and glimpsed other ways of living long before I ever left home. That early curiosity has steered almost every big decision I have made since.

My education started at the village primary school. Altınyurt had no middle school, so each morning a transport programme took me to a school in a nearby town. It was my first small journey out into the wider world. Later, at Gaziantep High School, I found myself drawn to global affairs, and above all to the Middle East, a region I have always seen as a boiling cauldron. Studying it became a dream, and that interest led me to study International Relations, taught in English, at Yalova University. I graduated in 2017.

Then came a turning point. In the same year I graduated, I was awarded a government scholarship under Türkiye's 1416 Law (YLSY), which gave me the chance to continue my studies abroad. It was one of the happiest moments of my life, the moment the worlds I had only seen in films and books suddenly felt within reach. In May 2018, I moved to Iran. I began with intensive Persian training at the Dehkhoda Lexicon Institute and the International Center for Persian Studies, and went on to complete my master's in International Relations at the University of Tehran in 2021. My master's thesis examined how political considerations affect the implementation of legal agreements between Turkey and Iran.

After Iran, a new chapter began. In June 2023, I started my PhD in Sociology at the University of Groningen in the Netherlands. There I found myself in a very different academic world, more theoretical and built around new quantitative methods, and it gave me a whole new way of looking at the questions I care about. My research explores how social movements respond to repression, and how political and structural conditions shape when and how people act together. My current work focuses on the emotions of protesters and the role these emotions play in protest. My cases come from Iran, but my broader ambition is to build my career as a specialist in social movements. Through the SCOOP programme and ICS, I work in an interdisciplinary environment alongside researchers from very different fields. This has greatly enriched my work and taught me to look at protest, repression and politics from new angles.

Studying abroad turned that childhood curiosity into everyday life. I have lived among people from different cultures, made friends across borders, and learned their languages along the way. I believe that to truly understand a society, you need to understand its language, and learning a new one is one of the things that makes me happiest.

Outside academia, films are my greatest passion. Some of my favourites are listed above.

Living in different worlds changes a person, and it has certainly changed me. My curiosity has taken me from a small village out into the wider world, into very different cultures and societies. Each of these worlds has its own language, its own way of life and its own sense of what is right. I used to believe there was only one truth. Meeting different people, learning their languages and seeing how differently they live, believe and find happiness taught me that there are many.

Looking back, I could never have imagined that a child from a small village in Gaziantep would one day live in different countries and write a PhD on protest and social movements. The journey has taught me persistence, curiosity and the value of never stopping learning. Along the way, films, books and languages have remained my constant companions, still opening new worlds to me, just as they did when I was a child in the village.

“Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— / I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference.”Robert Frost

My path has rarely been the expected one, and I am still curious to see where it leads next.
